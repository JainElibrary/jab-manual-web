## Shrimad Räjchandra 

### Introduction

Shrimad Räjchandra (1867 to 1901 A.D.) is one of the most recognized
Jain legends of modern times, having made priceless contributions to
Jain philosophy and literature. He was a great philosopher, brilliant
poet, social reformer, and thinker. Mahatma Gandhi was highly influenced
by Shrimad's spiritual life. Shrimad's writings come from his
experiences and his teachings are widely followed by many Jain and Hindu
devotees seeking spiritual awakening and self-realization.

### Birth and Childhood

Shrimad Räjchandra was born on the auspicious full-moon (Dev-Diwäli) of
the month of Kärtak, 1924 V.S. (November 9, 1867) in Vaväniä, Morbi
district, Gujarat. He was born to a Hindu father and a Jain mother,
Ravajibhai and Devabä, respectively. Named Laxminandan at birth, his
name was changed to Raichand after four years. Later, he became famous
by the name of Räjchandra.

Räjchandra's father and grandfather followed the Vaishnav (Hindu)
religion. They were devotees of Lord Krishna. His mother, Devabä, was
from a Jain family. Thus, the child Räjchandra was brought up under the
unified culture of Jainism and Hinduism. Shrimad also had a younger
brother named Shri Mansukhbhai.

Räjchandra was extraordinary from his early life. As a child, he was
deeply interested in reading various books. Once he read the Jain
Pratikraman sutras, he was touched by feelings of compassion towards
animals and the expression of sincere forgiveness towards others. He was
drawn to the Jain faith because of its emphasis on self-knowledge,
self-control, penance, renunciation, detachment from worldly affairs,
and meditation. As a seeker of ultimate truth, Shrimad concluded that
the philosophy and culture of Jain religion was of the highest order of
truth and equanimity.

An incident that took place when he was seven years old, changed the
course of Shrimad's life. An adult acquaintance, Shri Amichandbhai, died
of a serpent bite. Young Räjchandra asked his grandfather, "What does it
mean to die?" His loving grandfather explained, "His soul has left the
body, and he will no longer be able to eat, talk, or move. His body will
be burnt outside the village."

Räjchandra saw the dead body being cremated and slipped into deep
contemplation. Suddenly, as if a veil lifted, he recalled his past lives
and achieved Jäti-smaran Jnän. He realized the pains of repeated births
and deaths from one life to another, making this incidence a major
spiritual awakening point in his life. It gave him a deeper
understanding of karma, reincarnation, and detachment.

At the same age, Räjchandra started school. With his exceptional ability
to remember exactly what he saw, heard, or read once; he was able to
complete the seven-year elementary school education in only two years.
Although his formal education had ended, he continued to learn and read
many books on his own. He also worked in his father's shop, conducting
honest and sincere business.

He composed his first poem when he was 8 and subsqeuently began writing
poems and articles related to social issues for publication in local
newsletters. Despite his young age, he drafted serious articles on
subjects like the need for improving female literacy rates, ending child
marriages, and the excessive display of wealth by the rich. He also
composed poems with nationalistic zeal.

Räjchandra also developed the supernatural ability to predict what was
likely to happen in his youth, helping others by saving them from
possible disasters. At the age of 16, he wrote "Moksh-Mälä," which
describes Jain principles and teachings as the true guide to the path of
detachment. By 18, he became a very proficient astrologer. He was able
to identify books merely by touching them, as well as know the taste of
food without tasting it. Alongside all this, he became very
compassionate towards all living beings and was strong promoter of
non-violence.

### Family

In 1888 A.D., at the age of 20, Räjchandra married Zabakben. She was the
daughter of Shri Popatlalbhai Jagjivandas, the elder brother of Shri
Revashankarbhai (who later became Shrimad's business partner). They had
four children: sons Chhaganlal and Ratiläl, and daughters Javalben and
Käshiben.

Shrimad Räjchandra became a partner in a gemstone business in Mumbai
that same year. He was ethical, honest, and compassionate in all his
business dealings. His business shortly flourished because of his wisdom
and business insight. His regard for truth, adherence to high moral
values, and dedication to doing the right thing inspired many others. In
1899 A.D., at the age of 31, he retired from business.

### Power of Avadhän (Multi-Tasking)

At the age of 17, Shrimad had observed someone performing eight
different tasks simultaneously, known as Ashtävadhäni in Gujarati. He
studied this method and was able to perform twelve tasks the next day.
He soon improved his performance to be able to manage 52 Avadhäns.

At the age of 19, he displayed his ability to remember and answer 100
questions in an order called "Shatävadhän", performaed at Faramji
Kavasji Institute in Mumbai in the presence of the Bombay Chief Justice
and other dignitaries.

The 100 tasks included a variety of activities like playing cards,
playing chess, counting the bell chimes, completing mathematical
calculations, composing poems with different themes and specified sets
of words, and arranging the order of words from 16 different languages
including English, Greek, Latin and Arabic. Shrimad's formal study only
included Gujarati up to Grade 7.

This was an unbelievable feat, and the performance was publicized in
major newspapers, such as "Times of India" and "Pioneer". Shrimad was
invited to go to Europe and demonstrate his extraordinary talents, which
would have meant more fame and substantial material gain.

However, he declined the offer because it would be extremely difficult
to live in accordance with Jain principles in Europe. He also knew he
would become distracted from his main objective of spiritual progress.

By the time he was 20, he realized that he was achieving only material
benefits from displaying his amazing powers, which was not what he
wanted from life. He gave up all such activities and concentrated on
self-restraint, detachment from worldly matters, contemplation, and
meditation. His intention was to progress spiritually and free himself
from the cycle of birth and death.

### Shrimad's Writings

In his early years, Shrimad composed poems and articles on social
reform. When working in his father's shop, he completed an in-depth
study of the Jain Ägams and scriptures after learning Sanskrit and
Präkrit on his own at fourteen.

When he was16, he wrote "Moksh-mälä" and its compendium "Bhävanä-bodh,"
which means "garland of liberation". True to its name, it deals with
subjects that lead towards the path of liberation. Although written
simply, it explains the Jain religion in great detail. Its 108 lessons
were composed in three days!

"Ätmasiddhi-shästra", translating to 'Attainment of the Soul,' is a
Gujarati epic poem and the prime jewel composed by Shrimad in 1896 while
he was in Nadiad (a town near Ahmedäbäd).

On one auspicious evening, while sitting with his friend Sobhägbhai, he
composed a short verse (142 stanzas) of Ätmasiddhi-shästra in less than
90 minutes, summarizing his view of Jainism. The fact that Shrimad
composed such a comprehensive and all-inclusive work in such a short
time speaks to the depth of spiritual wealth he possessed.

The subject matter of Ätmasiddhi-shästra is the scientific
characterization of the six-fold truths of soul: The soul exists, the
soul is eternal, the soul is the doer of its actions, the soul
experiences the fruits of its actions, the soul's nature is that of pure
consciousness, and the soul holds the potential for attaining liberation
from the cycle of birth and death.

It comprehensively describes the Jain philosophy and shows how the
principles of Anekäntaväd allow Jainism to encompass all other Indian
faiths. Many scholars have since written elegant commentaries on the
"Ätmasiddhi-shästra".

"Apurva Avasar," his last major compilation, was incorporated into the
prayer book in Gandhiji's Ashram. It is his most divine poem, in which
Shrimad expounds the fourteen stages of spiritual progress for ultimate
liberation.

Shrimad's complete works contain more than 35 poems and between 800 and
950 letters written to various people he interacted with. They are
compiled in a text called "Vachanämrit." If one delves deeper into his
literature, they will notice that his writings provide excellent
discourses on attaining self-realization and progress along the path to
liberation.

He also wrote many small books, such as Bhävanä-bodh,
Sukh-sambandhi-vichar, and Namiräj. Mahatma Gandhi is one example of a
prominent personality who was very impressed by Shrimad's spirituality
and lifestyle. He respected and revered Shrimad as his spiritual guide.
When Gandhiji was in South Africa, he was under intense pressure from
his Christian and Muslim friends to adopt their faith. He wrote to
Shrimad for guidance, which convinced him that his own faith, Hinduism,
would ensure his spiritual progress.

Gandhiji wrote about Shrimad in his autobiography and paid glorious
tributes to him on several occasions. He often expressed that he learned
the principles of non-violence and compassion towards all beings from
Shrimad.

According to him, Shrimad's life and spiritual writings impacted him
more than those of Tolstoy and Ruskin.

### Spiritual Progress

Shrimad did not have a spiritual teacher in his life. However, at the
age of seven, when he was able to recall his past lives, he clearly
remembered his association with Bhagawän Mahävir in a previous life.

In 1891 A.D., at age 23, it is believed that Shrimad attained Samyag
Darshan. He continued to enhance his spiritual progress by slowly
detaching himself from the material world, deeply studying scriptures,
strengthening his virtues, refraining from worldly pleasures, and
meditating for long durations of time. He started to stay in isolated
places outside Mumbai for months at a time.

Initially, Shrimad faced many obstacles doing so as he also needed to
perform his duties as a householder and businessman.

From 1896 A.D. onwards, he often spent several months alone in the woods
of Uttarsanda, Idar, and Kavitha, where he would eat only one meal
during the day and sleep very little. Rather, he spent his time in deep
meditation, and is believed to have attained a highly enlightened state
of self-realization.

He retired from business at age 31 in 1899 A.D. and asked his mother for
permission to renounce his worldly life by taking Diskha (vows of
renunciation). Unfortunately, his mother denied his request out of love
and affection.

After two years of clever persuasion, he was hopeful of obtaining his
mother's approval, but by this time his health had significantly
deteriorated. He died in 1901 A.D. in Rajkot at the young age of 33.

### Followers

Though Shrimad tried to keep his spiritual personality private, several
people accepted him as a guide to achieving ultimate liberation,
essentially devoting their lives to him. Some of his closest followers
were:

#### Shri Sobhägbhai

Almost 350 of Shrimad Räjchandra's letters were written to Shri
Sobhägbhai, who was about 40 years older than him. In the very first
meeting, Sobhägbhai recognized Shrimad as a highly self-realized person
and accepted him as his true guru. He was a simple and devoted man
hailing from Saila, a town near Rajkot. Shrimad composed the epic poem
"Ätmasiddhi-shästra" upon his request to make it easier to memorize.
Shri Sobhägbhai is believed to have attained self-realization and died
in deep meditation (Samädhi).

#### Shri Laghuräj Swämi

Shri Laghuräj Swämi was a Sthänakaväsi Jain monk and one of Shrimad's
most devoted disciples. Given that he was a monk, his devotion to
Shrimad was considered problematic by some in the Jain community.

Shri Laghuräj Swämi attained self-realization in Shrimad's presence and
then established the Agäs Äshram near Vadodara. The äshram holds credit
for preserving Shrimad's writings and making it available to the larger
Jain community. Agäs is a prominent place for Shrimad's followers -
today, there are more than fifty äshrams in India and abroad, where
followers worship and study Shrimad's literature based on the divine
faith and testimony provided by Shri Laghuräj Swämi.

#### Shri Ambälälbhai

Ambälälbhai, a native of Khambhät, was another devoted disciple who
sacrificed a brilliant legal career to serve Shrimad. Shrimad entrusted
him to copy scriptural works and his letters because of his exceptional
memory. He and Shrimad's younger brother, Mansukhbhai, were responsible
for recovering and publishing Shrimad's letters and writings.

Four years after Shrimad's death, Ambälälbhai is believed to have
attained self-realization, and died in deep Samädhi in 1905 A.D.

#### Shri Joothäbhai

Joothäbhai was the first person to recognize Shrimad's divinity and
accept him as his spiritual mentor. Their relationship was very close,
but also very short as Shri Joothäbhai died when he was only 23 years
old in 1890 A.D.

### Teachings and Contribution of Shrimad Räjchandra

All of Shrimad's literature is based on Bhagwän Mahävir's teachings. He
presented these teachings in simple Gujarati and Hindi, in both prose
and poetry forms.

Studying Shrimad's writings in depth reveals that he made an enormous
effort to shed new light on true spirituality. He has succeeded in
guiding those with blind faith to spiritual awakening.

His biggest contribution is to point out the mistakes most people make
when they accept someone as a teacher based solely on their external
conduct and dress code.

The spiritual journey led by the wrong teacher leads a disciple to
extended life cycles of misery and pain. On the other hand, when an
aspirant knows and follows the teachings of a true teacher (sadguru), he
will be able to attain true freedom and liberation.

#### Morality

Good manners, good activities, and good behavior are the roots of
holiness.

All living beings are seen as equal. Therefore, do not cause pain to any
soul, nor extort work from any soul beyond their ability and capacity.

#### Human Life

All living beings long for permanent happiness with no exception. This
desire can be fulfilled only in a human life. Yet human beings choose
unhappiness due to one's illusionary state, (Mohaniya karma - Mithyätva)
in which one finds happiness in worldly desires and possessions.

#### Non-attachment to Worldly Affairs (Vairägya)

Detachment from worldly and materialistic affairs and family
relationships is defined as Vairägya, and achieving it is essential for
eternal happiness.

True renunciation is necessary to gain right knowledge of the self or
the soul, which is otherwise not possible.

#### Knowledge and Wisdom

Through proper knowledge, we are able to know the forms and qualities of
the substances of the universe.

The Jain scripture, Uttarädhyayan Sutra, indicates that "Knowledge is
like a needle with thread. Just like a needle with a thread in it does
not get lost, a person with knowledge will not lose the right religious
path in this world." It is the true knowledge with which an external
feeling is controlled, the attachment for life, family and worldly
pleasures decreases, and the truth is revealed.

If you know yourself, you know the universe. If you do not know
yourself, your knowledge is worthless.

### Summary

Shrimad Räjchandra was a great saint, a self-realized master, and an
outstanding educator. He was a born poet, possessed an extraordinary
power of memory, a reformer of society, a keen follower of Jainism, and
an advocate of non-violence. He had equal respect for all religions.

He emphasized that he did not belong to any Gachchha, or sect, but only
to his soul. He believed that the nineteenth century decline of Jainism
was due to excessive sectarianism and temple rituals. However, Shrimad
Räjchandra later accepted that idol worship is an aid to spiritual
growth.

As with many great personalities, Shrimad's greatness was not recognized
in his lifetime. Shrimad was not popular among Jains because he spoke
about faults in Jain society and traditions being carried out without
understanding the proper meaning and purpose behind them. Many people
recognized his greatness after his death.

Shrimad put great emphasis on the need of a Sadguru in one's life in
order to make spiritual progress and ultimately to attain liberation.
Shrimad maintained that since he had not taken the vow of Dikshä
(sainthood), he was not eligible to preach the path of liberation.

Therefore, even with his wealth of knowledge, he did not preach to the
masses. He hoped that later in his life, he would become a monk, and
when the time was right, he could bring the proper teachings of Jainism
to others, as there were many undesired factors in the practice of
Jainism at that time. Unfortunately, he lived a very short life.

However, his work survives and continues to change the lives of many
through religious centers established by his followers.

Moral:

Shrimad's writings to his close associates and his personal notes
explain the essence of Jain religion. He has given us priceless
spiritual gifts, including "Moksh-mälä", "Ätmasiddhi-shästra", and
"Apurva Avasar", as well as other writings in the form of letters,
articles, and poems. In his short life of 33 years, he delivered
spiritual messages of eternal importance, explaining Bhagawän Mahävir's
teachings simply. Now, these teachings have become accessible to
ordinary people, and give us the unique opportunity to learn about the
inner state of a truly self-realized person.
