## Anekäntaväd I: Theory of Multiplicity

### Introduction

Modern day logic is defined as the study of principles and method of
argumentation. An argument in the system of logic is a set of
statements.

The history of Jain logic and Jain epistemology goes as far back as its
canonical literature. We find the doctrines and discussions, as well as
reasoning on those doctrines, even in the philosophical works by Ächärya
Umäsväti and Kundkund Ächärya.

The Nyäyävatära by Shri Siddhasen Diwäkar, as far as we know, is the
earliest manual on logic composed for the benefit and training of Jain
authors. Until his time, they studied Nyäya possibly from other sources
available to them.

Shvetämbar Ächärya Siddhasen Diwäkar has been accepted as 'the first
Jain writer on pure logic.' Between the 5th and 16th centuries some
noteworthy Jain logicians from Siddhasen to Yashovijayaji include
Mallavädi, Haribhadra-suri, Akalank, Virsen, Vidyänandi, Devasuri, and
Ächärya Hemchandra.

Philosophical and religious arguments about the nature and origin of
reality are as old as human history. In India, sages and philosophers
held many metaphysical views and were in constant dialogue and argument
with one another. Jains were active participants in debates, and among
their central tenets was the position referred to as Anekäntaväd.
Translated literally, it means the multiplicity and relativity of
viewpoints.

Anekäntaväd means that in many cases the arguments adopted by various
participants in a debate all hold some validity. From the Jain
standpoint it is possible to overcome the apparent inconsistencies
between other views since it comes closer to fully grasping the one with
underlying truth.

Let us take an example. My name is Kirit. My father's name is Prabhudas
and my son's name is Amit. Now I am a father and son at the same time.
How can this be? From Prabhudas' perspective, I am a son and from Amit's
perspective, I am a father. Thus, both statements are true from their
own perspectives.

Here is another case: we know that the soul is eternal as well as
changing. How can these two conflicting statements be true? According to
Jain logic, they are true statements from their own perspective. Soul is
eternal from a substantial point of view (Dravya), but it is ever
changing from a modal point of view (Paryäy).

Jain literature states that "non-absolutism is the principal dogma of
Jainism". Therefore, "every statement is to be accepted as relative
truth".

In this way, Anekäntaväd maintains that the truth has many facets. Each
viewpoint may be true from one perspective while not so from the other.
Furthermore, only the omniscient know the whole truth. Worldly beings
without omniscience are limited in their capacity to know and comprehend
the whole truth. Anekäntaväd is elegantly demonstrated in the following
story.

An Elephant and The Blind Men

Once upon a time, there lived six blind men in a village. One day the
villagers told them, "Hey, there is an elephant in the village today."
They had no idea what an elephant looked like, since they were blind.
They decided, however, to go and feel the elephant anyway. Each of them
touched the elephant.

"Hey, the elephant is like a pillar," said the first man, who touched
its leg.

"Oh, no! It is like a rope," said the second man, who touched tits tail.

"Oh, no! It is like a python," said the third man, who touched its
trunk. "It is like a big hand fan" said the fourth man, who touched tits
ear.

"It is like a drum," said the fifth man, who touched its belly.

"It is like a solid pipe," said the sixth man, who touched its tusk.

They began to argue about the elephant, with each insisting that he was
right. They became agitated. A wise man saw this while passing by. He
stopped and asked them, "What is the matter?" "We cannot agree on what
the elephant is like", they said. Everyone offered their perception. The
wise man calmly explained, "All of you are right. The reason each one of
you is perceiving the elephant differently is because each of you
touched a different part of the elephant."

"Oh!" they said. There was no more fighting. They felt happy and content
that they were all right. Any statement regarding the truth may be true
in its own limited way. So, rather than arguing like the blind men, we
should say, "Maybe you have your reasons." In this ever-changing
universe, an infinite number of viewpoints exist. These viewpoints
depend on the time, place, circumstances, and nature of individuals.

According to Jain metaphysics, innumerable material, and spiritual
substances, both of which are the locus of innumerable qualities,
constitute reality. Not only are there innumerable substances, each with
innumerable quality, but each quality is susceptible to an infinite
number of modifications.

Non-omniscients cannot comprehend this complex reality, for ordinary
knowledge is limited not only by the limited power of the senses and
reason, but also by the perspectives adopted by the knower as well as by
the conditions of space, time, light, and so on.

The six blind men could have been right from their own perspective. They
felt that the elephant could be a pillar, long rope, python, huge fan,
drum, and a solid pipe from the individual consideration of the legs,
tail, trunk, ear, belly, and tusk, respectively.

Therefore, if you do not have complete knowledge nor believe in other
possibilities and think that the partial point of view is the only truth
and others are wrong, then your partial point of view is incorrect.

Thus, an understanding of Jain logic helps develop tolerance. Nothing is
absolutely wrong nor is it absolutely right. All the statements are true
from their own perspective. We cannot have complete knowledge of a
substance because of our inability to know substance as a whole. Only
omniscient Bhagwän have perfect and complete knowledge.

The spoken and written language has many limitations. So, one has to
understand the broader meaning of Jain logic and then try to understand
reality from that perspective. We should strive to know all the angles
of the substance and then present a partial point of view in context of
that complete view. Presenting the partial point of view, and then
considering it as complete knowledge is wrong according to Jain logic.

We should also keep in mind that when a sentence is spoken, we should
consider the angle from which it is spoken. If we understand the
perspective in which it was based correctly, then our knowledge base
increases. Literature is also written either in a substantial point of
view (Dravyärthik Naya) or a modal point of view (Paryäyärthik Naya).

Thus, to achieve complete knowledge (Pramän Jnän), we should also know
the partial points of view (Naya) composing the truth. The partial
viewpoints become pillars on which the building of complete knowledge
rests. Of course, the true and complete knowledge of a substance is only
possible with omniscience. Thus, in our current circumstances, we should
strive to develop as complete of a worldview as possible. While it may
not be perfect, understanding greater partial viewpoints is advantageous
to understanding fewer ones.

### Tripadi

Shraman Bhagawän Mahävir expounded and established the Jain philosophy
in three phrases known as Tripadi. These three phrases which constitute
the foundation of the Jain philosophy; and lays down its essentials are:

#### Uppannei Vä -- Utpäd (Origination)

There emerges a new phase of the substance. This is called Utpäd or
Utpatti, which denotes emergence of a new mode.

#### Vigamei Vä -- Vyaya (Destruction)

The old mode of the substance vanishes. This is called Vyaya or Laya,
which denotes the disappearance of the old mode.

#### Dhuvei Vä -- Dhrauvya (Permanence)

The original qualities of the substance remain constant. This is called
Dhrauvya, which denotes the permanence of the substance.

Though the substance may assume different forms at different times, it
never loses its own essential qualities (Guna). The Jain term for
substance is Sat (existence, being). This term denotes a substance that
has three aspects: the substance itself known as Dravya, its attributes
or qualities known as Guna, and various modes known as Paryäya.

### Dravya, Guna, and Paryäya

The substance and attributes are inseparable, and the attributes being
the permanent essence of a substance, cannot remain without it. Modes,
on the other hand, are constantly changing. The matter (Dravya), while
retaining its own qualities, undergoes modifications (Parinäm) in the
form of acquiring (Utpäd) new modes (Paryäya or Bhäva) and losing
(Vyaya) old modes at each moment. Production (acquiring new modes) and
destruction (losing old modes) are endless processes. Despite these
changes, the substance does not experience any loss in its original
qualities (Guna).

Substance as Dravya remains permanent and is not destroyable. For this
reason, Jainism does not consider any substance either as always
permanent or as always transitory. The destruction of anything that we
notice is not the destruction of the substance. It is only a change of
mode, a transformation.

Examples of Dravya, Guna, and Paryäya

A bar of gold has its own original qualities. That bar can be converted
into a chain. In that case, the shape of the bar is destroyed, and a new
shape (chain) is produced. However, the qualities of gold remain
unchanged. Now if we melt the chain and make a bangle out of it, then we
destroy the chain (an old form) and produce a bangle (a new form).
Again, the inherent qualities of the gold remain unchanged. Therefore,
the bar, the chain and the bangle are transient forms (Paryäya) while
gold is the matter (Dravya), which remains constant.

A living being through the process of growth undergoes various changes
such as childhood, youth, and old age. These changes are the natural
modifications of the living being. Childhood, youth, and old age are
transient forms (Paryäya) of a living being. The soul of the living
being is a permanent substance (Dravya). Similarly, when we die, we will
be born in another body. Therefore, the body is also a transient form
while our soul is the permanent substance (Dravya).

A soul is a substance (Dravya) that has innumerable qualities such as
knowledge (Jnän), bliss (Änand) and energy (Virya). The quality of
knowledge, for example, may increase or decrease but there is never a
time when the soul is without knowledge.

According to Jainism, the number of various substances existing at
present, those that existed in the past, and those that will continue to
exist in future, remains constant. There cannot be any increase or
decrease in that number. All the transformations take place according to
their properties and potential, and in the course of time, one form may
get destroyed and cease to exist and another form may emerge. However,
Dravya remains constant.

Jainism points out that both, permanence, and the change, are the two
sides of the same thing. Consider that on one side are the human
limitations to acquire the knowledge of a thing with all its infinite
attributes and on the other side are the three characteristics of
knowledge possessing the three characteristics of production,
destruction, and permanence. Nothing could be affirmed absolutely as all
affirmations could be relatively true under certain aspects or points of
view only. The affirmations are true of a thing only in a certain
limited sense and not absolutely.

Thus, a thing or the conception of being as the union of permanence and
change brings us naturally to the doctrine of Anekäntaväd or what we may
call relative pluralism. The claim that Anekäntaväd is the most
consistent form of realism lies in the fact that Jainism has allowed the
principle of distinction to run its full course until it reaches its
logical terminus, the theory of manifoldness of reality and knowledge.

### Application of Anekäntaväd

-   Develop a strong urge to seek truth.

-   Believe in many possibilities.

-   Do not only insist on your own approach.

-   Accept partial truth as expressed by others.

-   Accept the truth even if expressed by adversaries.

-   Accept that the truth can consist of seemingly opposing views.

-   Exercise equanimity towards all

The principle of Anekäntaväd, along with other Jain principles of Ahimsä
and Aparigraha, have great relevance in modern times if faithfully
adhered to in its right perspective.

These principles can bring contentment, inner happiness, and joy in the
present life through spiritual development based on freedom from
passions and kindness towards all beings. Nonviolence (Ahimsä)
strengthens the autonomy of life everywhere, non-absolutism
(Anekäntaväd) strengthens autonomy of thoughts & speech, and
non-possessiveness (Aparigraha) strengthens autonomy of interdependence.

These three realistic principles, in turn, strengthen our belief that
every living being has a right to self-existence. Three of several
practices that these principles translate into are:

-   One should not kill.

-   One should not trample other's thoughts.

-   One should not trample the natural world.

We can say that following these principles can:

-   Establish universal friendship and peace through nonviolence.

-   Establish true social equity based on non-acquisitiveness and
    non-possession.

-   Reconcile differences between diverse religious faiths, political
    parties, and communal and racial factions through the philosophies
    of pluralism, non-absolutism, and relativism.

-   Promote ecological conservation through the values of
    self-restraint, an austere lifestyle, and non-possessiveness.

### Knowing a Substance

To know a substance, one must understand it through 4 different
categories, as described in scriptures.

-   Characteristics (Lakshan)

-   True Knowledge (Pramän)

-   Partial point of view (Naya)

-   Analysis of truth (Nikshep)

#### Lakshan (Characteristics of a Substance)

To know a substance, one must know its characteristics (Lakshan), which
should be such that it is present only in that substance and not in any
other substance.

For example, while the soul is formless, this is not an absolute
characteristic of the soul because other substances (like the medium of
motion, medium of rest, space, and time) are also formless. However, if
we say that the soul's characteristic is 'to know,' that would be a true
characteristic. Every soul, starting with the lowest forms (Nigod) to
the highest forms (Siddha), has characteristics of knowledge. In another
application, touch, taste, smell, and color are all characteristics of
matter because no other Dravya (eternal substances) has these
characteristics.

Thus, a unique characteristic (present in only one substance and not in
any others) is known as a true characteristic of that substance.

#### Pramän (True Knowledge)

Pramän is the complete knowledge of the self and non-self without
limitations. To know a substance from all angles is called true
knowledge. Praman allows one to differentiate and make decisions about
the self and others by granting the full, proper perception of a
substance's nature. With an increase in true knowledge, doubt, illusion,
and ignorance are removed and the nature of a thing is understood
correctly to a considerable extent. Thus, with true knowledge, one
achieves equanimity and becomes tolerant of different viewpoints.

Pramän is gained by direct (Pratyaksha) or indirect (Paroksha) means.
Avadhi Jnän, Manah-paryav Jnän and Keval-jnän are examples of direct
Pramän. Indirect Pramän is gained by sensory organs and by reading and
listening to discourses. Thus, Mati Jnän and Shrut-jnän are examples of
indirect Pramän.

#### Naya (Partial Point of View)

Any knowledge that is only true for a given situation or from a given
point of view is called Naya (a partial point of view). The thought
activity, which grasps only one aspect of an object with the aid of
scriptures, is called a partial point of view.

Naya being a particular standpoint determines only a part of its object.
A Naya can also be defined as a particular intention or viewpoint, which
does not rule out other viewpoints and is thereby expressive of a
partial truth about an object as entertained by a knowing agent or
speaker. Nayas do not interfere with one another, instead they uphold
their own objectives without rejecting others' objects.

A Naya becomes a pseudo-Naya, when it denies all standpoints,
contradicts them, excludes them absolutely and puts forward its partial
truth as the whole truth.

A statement made from one point of view can never be true from all
aspects. Absolute truth cannot be grasped from any one particular
viewpoint. Absolute truth is the total sum of individual partial truths
from many different viewpoints, even if they seem to contradict each
other.

Like the blind men, each person perceives things from their own
perspective. These perspectives are determined by many factors,
including socio-cultural conditioning, place, time, circumstances,
hopes, and fears; each view is also, of course, subject to the
limitation of one's sensory perception and reasoning power.

When it is understood that knowledge is limited by the particular
perspective from which it is achieved, it becomes easy to see that
claims of knowledge are conditioned by the limitation of the perspective
that it assumes and should always be expressed as only tentatively true.
Just as the blind men should have been more circumspect, saying, for
example, "Standing here, feeling the object with my hands, it feels like
a winnowing fan - this elephant may look like a winnowing fan," we must
understand that any claims of knowledge should be asserted only
conditionally.

Complete knowledge (Pramän or Jnän) is the total of all partial points
of view. Thus, to understand a substance in its fullest form, one must
have knowledge of all partial points of view, including seemingly
opposite partial points of view. Pramän and Naya both constitute pure
knowledge, but the former grasps it in entirety, while the latter grasps
only one of its many aspects.

There are several different classifications of partial points of view
given in scriptures. Details can be found in the next chapter.

#### Nikshep (Analysis of Truth)

An analysis of truth can be done with precision and clarity in several
ways. A substance has various attributes. Keeping those attributes in
mind, a substance can be assessed through different channels.

Language allows intangible knowledge to become tangible and hence
conveyable. However, the same word can yield several meanings depending
on purpose or context. Employment of words to express different meanings
can be done in at least four different ways, which are known as Nikshep.

Four Nikshep (Four Way Analysis of Truth) are:

##### Näm (Name)

The meaning that is not derived etymologically but is gathered on the
basis of conventions set up by members of society (parents, teachers,
etc.) is known as Näm Nikshep. It refers to the object merely by its
name, often making daily activities easier. For example, a poor person's
name might be Raja. He is known as Raja by name, even though he is very
poor.

##### Sthäpanä (Symbol)

Sthäpanä means to refer to a person through his/her image, idol,
picture, painting, etc. These things contain an impression of the
original object; for example, looking at a marble idol in a temple, one
may say it is Mahävir Swämi. In this usage, we consider the real thing
and its representation (a statue, a photograph, or a picture) to be one
and the same.

##### Dravya (Potentiality)

Here, one refers to an object by mentioning its past or future
conditions. The term 'Dravya' in the word 'Dravya Nikshep' conveys a
sense of potentiality. For example, we might refer to a prince as a king
now, even though he is not currently, but he will be a king in the
future.

##### Bhäv (Actuality)

Bhäv is the name signifying an object's meaning in its present
condition, satisfying the etymology of the concerned word. A person is
called a king when he is actually carrying the royal scepter and is
shining with glory on that account; in this present condition, he is
king in a real sense.

For example, the word Tirthankar is used only after the soul attains
omniscience and is now preaching Jain Dharma and establishing a fourfold
religious congregation.

Thus, Nikshepa of a word helps in arriving at its correct meaning, and
at the same time it helps in explaining how the particular word is used
at a particular place. These are aids to the application of Naya and
sometimes considered as part and parcel of the same.

### Summary -- Anekäntaväd 

We worship Bhagawän by respectfully remembering and saying their names,
worshipping their images, and worshipping them by devotedly serving the
spiritual teacher because the real spiritual teacher can be regarded as
Bhagawän in potential. In this way, Näm Nikshep, Sthäpanä Nikshep, and
Dravya Nikshep (or rather our activities performed with respect to these
three meanings) lead to Bhäv Nikshep (or the activity with respect to
the Bhäv Nikshep).
