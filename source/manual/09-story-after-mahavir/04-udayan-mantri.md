## Udayan Mantri and His Sons: Ämbad and Bähad

### Udayan Mantri (Minister)

The Solanki dynasty was the golden period for the state of Gujarat and
prosperity reached its peak during King Kumärpäl's reign. The credit for
this mainly goes to his chancellors, known as his "Mantri".
Interestingly, many of the chancellors of the Solanki period were Jains.
The contribution of Udayan Mantri and his sons, Ämbad and Bähad, to the
rise and success of King Kumärpäl was particularly substantial.

Udayan originated from the village of Vägharä, near the town of Jälore,
Rajasthan, where he was a simple merchant. He struggled to make ends
meet and his days passed in hardship. His wife, Suhädevi, suggested they
move to a place with better economic prospects. During this time, King
Siddharäj was ruling the state of Gujarat and its prosperity was
increasing rapidly. Hence, Udayan thought about migrating to Gujarat.

At that time, King Siddharäj had built a new town named Karnävati
(Amdäväd), in memory of his father Karnadev. Since Karnävati was a
fast-growing city, Udayan decided to move there. He did not know anyone
there, so he first went to the local Jain temple. When Udayan arrived, a
religious lady named Lachchhi was in the temple. She noticed the new
young couple when leaving and asked them where they were from. Udayan
replied that they were from Rajasthan and was looking for some business
in Karnävati.

Lachchhi was a compassionate lady. Knowing the newcomers were Jains, she
took them home and hosted them for a few days. Then, she gave them an
old house for shelter. Udayan settled there and started a small
business. Luck favored him, and within a brief time he earned and saved
enough to renovate the old house. During excavations, he came across a
hidden treasure. An honest man, he offered it to Lachchhi because it
belonged to her. She declined to accept it, stating that since she had
given them the property, the treasure was also theirs.

Udayan now had enough money to start a large-scale business. He made
lots of money, and in due course became the wealthiest man in Karnävati.
The state recognized his status and appointed him as the first citizen
of Karnävati. In that capacity, he rendered valuable service to the
townspeople.

At that time, the city of Khambhät (about 80 miles from Amdäväd) was the
most lucrative seaport on the western coast of India. Politicians
competed with one another to become governor of the city. Proving his
capabilty, Udayan was appointed governor of Khambhät in 1120 A.D., which
he held for a fairly long period. Following are two major events
attributed to him during his tenure as governor:

-   At the suggestion of the queen mother, Minaldevi, he abolished the
    pilgrim tax of Bholad.

-   He helped Devchandra-suri initiate a five-year old boy, Chängdev,
    who had the potential to shine as a great Jain monk. (Chängdev later
    became the great Jain Ächärya Shri Hemchandra)

His loyalty to the king was unquestionable. Once, he hesitated to help
King Kumärpäl, who was roaming from town to town hiding from King
Siddharäj. However, when Kumärpäl came to Khambhät in search of shelter,
Hemchandra Ächärya advised Udayan to help him. Since Udayan highly
respected the Ächärya, he agreed to hide Kumärpäl in his basement.
Shortly thereafter, King Siddharäj died and Kumärpäl became king of
Gujarat. King Kumärpäl kept Udayan in his position as governor of
Khambhät and later brought him to Pätan (capital city of Gujarat) to
work as his trusted advisor.

Udayan had not forgotten how religion had helped him during his tough
times. Therefore, he used his wealth and position to promote Jainism. As
a token of his devotion, he constructed several Jain temples. Three of
them are recorded in Jain history. One was the temple known as
Udayanvihär in Karnävati, the second was Udävasahi in Dholka (nearby
town of Karnävati), and the third one (whose name is not recorded) was
in Khambhät.

During the latter part of Udayan's life, Kumärpäl sent him to subdue
Sumvar, a notorious bandit in the Saurashtra region. In that mission, he
had to pass through Pälitänä. He decided to go on a pilgrimage to
Shatrunjay hill. To commemorate his pilgrimage, his statue was erected
on a camel's back in a small temple on the way to the main complex on
the hill. That place is now popularly known as Päp Punya Ni Bäri (window
of good and bad karma).

At that time, there was a wooden temple on the hill. While Udayan was
worshipping there, he saw a mouse take a lit wick in its mouth and roam
about. Udayan recovered the wick from the mouse, but realized how easily
the temple could be set on fire by a mouse at any moment. He therefore
vowed to construct a new temple there.

Udayan succeeded in his expedition against Sumvar but was badly wounded
in combat. His death imminent, he told his sons about his vow to
reconstruct a new temple at Shatrunjay hill on his deathbed. They
promised him that they would fulfill his vow, after which he died
peacefully.

### Ämbad and Bähad

Udayan had four sons, named Ämbad, Bähad, Chähad, and Sollak. Ämbad was
a poet and a bold warrior. He became the chancellor of King Kumärpäl. He
also constructed the western walkway on Shatrunjay hill, now known as
Gheti Ni Päg. Ämbad expanded Udävasahi of Dholka and renovated the
well-known temple of Shakunikävihär at Bharuch.

Ämbad pledged his allegiance to Kumärpäl and served him with utmost
sincerity and loyalty. When Kumärpäl's successor, King Ajaypäl, came to
the throne, he began to undo everything Kumärpäl had stood for. As a
result, Ämbad decided to resist him with all means at his command. King
Ajaypäl sent troops to subdue him. Ämbad died fighting Ajaypäl's troops.

The second son, Bähad (also known as Vähad), was a politician and
statesman. He first worked for King Siddharäj, and under King Kumärpäl's
regime became the king's trusted right-hand man. When Kumärpäl undertook
the renovation of Somnäth temple, the project was entrusted to Bähad,
who carried out the work very well.

To honor his pledge to his father, Bähad undertook construction of a
grand temple on Shatrunjay. The temple was completed in 1155 A.D., but
portions of the temple fell due to intense wind soon after. Bähad then
arranged to reconstruct the fallen parts so it could withstand high
winds in the future.

There is an interesting anecdote associated with the construction of
that temple. When Bähad undertook the work, many people wanted to
contribute to the project. A list of donors was prepared to accommodate
them. At that time, there was a poor man named Bhim who earned his
livelihood going place-to-place selling ghee (refined butter). He had an
intense desire to contribute to the project, but he had only one coin,
which he had earned that day. How could he speak about donating such a
small amount when people were contributing thousands of coins? Bähad
noticed his eagerness and called him to the side. He gently encouraged
Bhim to contribute whatever he wanted. Bhim hesitated at first. However,
he then said he wanted to donate the coin he earned that day.

Bähad not only accepted the offer, but also wrote Bhim's name at the top
of the donors' list. When asked to explain why, Bähad said that other
donors, including himself, had contributed part of their wealth, while
Bhim had contributed his entire wealth.

When Bhim returned home, his wife asked him to fix a wooden post to the
ground to restrain their cow. As he was digging, he came across a buried
box. Opening it, he found gold coins and other valuables. Thinking it
was the consequence of his contribution to the temple, he took the
treasure to Bähad as his contribution to the temple.

The construction project was completed in 1157 A.D. at a cost of 29.7
million coins, and the opening ceremony was performed on a grand scale
in the presence of Shri Hemchandra Ächärya.

Moral:

The life story of Udayan, a religious and hardworking Shrävak, is very
inspiring to us all. He was always humble and never forgot his
benefactors. He raised brave and virtuous sons, Ämbad and Bähad, two
gems of the Jain Sangh. Bhim's generosity is also praiseworthy. Bähad's
act of putting Bhim's name at the top of the donors' list demonstrates
his unbiased leadership and true understanding of religion.

Highlights:

-   Udayan was a minister in the court of King Kumärpäl.

-   When he was governor of Khambhät, he sheltered King Kumärpäl and
    later became his minister.

-   He asked his son to construct a new temple on Shatrunjay, since the
    old temple was made of wood and could be set on fire.

-   His son Bähad constructed one temple in 1155 A.D., but parts of it
    fell due to high winds.

-   He decided to reconstruct it and completed it in 1157 A.D.

-   At the top of the donor list was a poor man named Bhim who donated
    his entire wealth (even though it was only one coin).
