## Anekäntaväd II: Pramän, Naya and Syädväd

### Introduction

Logic broadly refers to the study of the structure and principles of
reasoning or sound arguments. In the west, it also encompasses other
meanings all related to different uses of the Greek word "logos". Logic
is of prime importance in Indian philosophy and religion. Thus, the
knowledge of logic is a must for one who wants to understand a religion
and its philosophy.

### Aim and Subject matter of Jain Logic

It is not possible to understand the scriptures and the doctrine without
the correct knowledge of Pramän (total view knowledge) and Naya (partial
viewpoint knowledge). Jain logic includes all such topics resulting from
Jain theory of knowledge and reality.

Pramän (true knowledge) in Jain philosophy is divided into two modes:
Pramän and Naya. Pramän is knowledge of a thing as it is, and Naya is
knowledge of a thing in its relation. Pramän comprises all the aspects
of a substance. Naya means a standpoint of thought from which we make a
statement about a thing. Siddhasen Diwäkar, in Nyäyävatära, writes,
"Since things have many characteristics, they are the object of complete
knowledge (omniscience), but a thing conceived from one particular point
of view is the object of Naya (or one-sided knowledge).'' As mentioned
above, Naya is a part of Pramän. This is because it gives us valid
knowledge of its object, albeit a partial knowledge.

### Pramän

Pramän is of two kinds:

-   Pratyaksha Pramän (Direct knowledge): Direct knowledge is that which
    is obtained by the soul without the help of external means. The
    Pratyaksha Pramän is of three kinds, namely Avadhi-jnän,
    Manah-paryav Jnän and Keval-jnän.

-   Paroksha Pramän (Indirect knowledge): Indirect knowledge is that
    which is obtained by the soul by means of external means, such as
    the five senses and the mind. Paroksha Pramän is classified into
    Mati-jnän and Shrut-jnän.

##### Sämvyavahärik Pratyaksha Pramän (Direct knowledge in a conventional sense)

-   Partial proper knowledge of a given substance acquired with the help
    of senses and mind is called Direct knowledge in the conventional
    sense (Sämvyavahärik Pratyaksha Pramän)

-   When the soul obtains right faith (Samyag Darshan), the sensory
    knowledge (Mati-jnän) and articulate knowledge (Shrut-jnän) are used
    for the knowledge of the self. Therefore, this is called direct
    knowledge in a conventional sense. Here the knowledge is partially
    true (Ekadesha Spashta)

##### Pärmärthik Pratyaksha Praman (Transcendental knowledge)

When the soul obtains direct knowledge without the help of any external
means (like the senses and mind), then it is called Päramärthik
Pratyaksha Pramän. It can be of two types:

-   Vikal Päramärthik or Partial perception

-   Sakal Päramärthik or Perfect perception

######  Vikal Päramärthik (Partial Perception Knowledge)

When the soul obtains direct knowledge of a formed substance without the
help of senses and mind, it is called partial perception knowledge or
Vikal Päramärthik. It is of two types:

-   Avadhi Jnän or Clairvoyance Knowledge

-   Manah-paryav Jnän or Telepathy

######  Sakal Päramärthik (Perfect Perception Knowledge)

Omniscience, which is knowledge with perfect perception, is often
referred to as Keval-jnän. Bhagwän, who has Keval-jnän (Sakal
Päramärthik), knows about all substances in the universe, and all of
their modes of past, present and future, at any given time. When a soul,
in one's quest for purity, destroys all four destructive (Ghäti) karma
at the 13th stage of the spiritual ladder, it obtains this omniscient
knowledge. This perfect knowledge stays with the soul forever.

#### Paroksha Pramän (Indirect Knowledge)

The knowledge that is not of the self is called indirect perception --
it is collected with the help of external means (like the five senses
and the mind). Partial knowledge obtained in such a way is therefore
called indirect knowledge (Paroksha Pramän). Paroksha Jnän is classified
into Mati-jnän and Shrut-jnän.

#### Types of Pramän

Thus, there are a total of five kinds of Pramän or Jnän or knowledge:
(1) Mati-jnän (2) Shrut-jnän (3) Avadhi-jnän (4) Manah-paryav jnän (5)
Keval-jnän.

##### Mati Jnän (Sensory Knowledge)

This knowledge is gained through the senses and/or mind. Reflection on
what has been perceived, reasoning, questioning, searching,
understanding, and forming judgements are varieties of sensory
knowledge. Mati Jnän can also be classified into the four categories of
remembrance, recognition, induction, and deduction.

-   Smaran (Remembrance)

-   Pratyabhijna (Recognition)

-   Tarka (Induction)

-   Anumän (Deduction)

##### Shrut Jnän (Scriptural Knowledge)

This knowledge refers to conceptualization through language. It is
obtained by studying scriptures and listening to discourses. Scriptural
knowledge (Ägam Knowledge) consists of comprehending words that are
heard or derived from the senses and the mind. This knowledge is
authoritative.

##### Avadhi Jnän (Clairvoyance)

Clairvoyance refers to knowledge of things that are out of the range of
senses. With Avadhi Jnän, the soul can perceive knowledge of a substance
with a form (Rupi Padärtha) that exists at great distance or at a
previous time. In celestial and infernal souls, this knowledge is
present from birth. In humans and animals, this knowledge can be
obtained as a result of spiritual endeavors.

##### Manah-paryav Jnän (Telepathy)

In this type of knowledge, the human soul has a capacity to comprehend
others' thoughts. Great saints who have achieved a high level of
spiritual progress can possess this knowledge.

##### Keval-jnän (Perfect Perception Knowledge)

Keval-jnän means exclusive prevalence of knowledge only and nothing else
and it is pure, untainted knowledge. This knowledge has infinite
capability to know everything, tangible or intangible, and for all time
in the past, present and future. About 'Keval-jnän', Dr. S.
Rädhäkrishnan writes: "It is omniscience unlimited by space, time or
object. To the perfect consciousness, the whole reality is obvious. This
knowledge, which is independent of the senses, and which can only be
felt and not described, is possible only for purified souls free from
bondage.''

### Summary of Pramän

Pramän or knowledge is capable of making us accept the agreeable things
and discard the disagreeable ones. The object of valid knowledge
according to Jains is always a unity of a number of aspects or
characteristics, such as general and particular, existent and the
nonexistent, etc.

It is also said that the immediate effect of Pramän is the removal of
ignorance; the immediate effect of absolute knowledge is bliss and
equanimity, while that of ordinary practical knowledge is the facility
to select or reject.

The subject of all forms of valid knowledge is the self, as known by
direct knowledge. The soul (Jiva) is the knower, doer, and enjoyer,
illumines self and others, undergoes changes of condition, is realized
only in self-consciousness, and as described in Jainism, is permanent.

Jainism asserts that knowledge attained is the knowledge of real
objects. What is known is not all aspects of the reality of an object,
but only one or some. In Jainism, knowledge depends on experience and
experience is always partial, in the sense that reality in totality is
never revealed. Under the circumstance, whatever is known, is known in
relation to a standpoint and therefore "absolutism is to be
surrendered.'' This is the root of Nayaväd and Syädväd.

### Naya

The term Anekäntaväd consists of three terms: 'Anek', 'Änta', and 'Väd'
The term 'Anek' means 'many or more than one', 'Änta' means 'aspects' or
'attributes', and 'Väd' means 'ism' or 'theory'. More simply, it is a
philosophy or a doctrine of manifold aspects.

It has been described and translated by modern scholars in a variety of
ways. Prof. S. N. Dasgupta expresses it as 'relative pluralism' against
'extreme absolutism'. Dr. Chandradhar Sharma translates it as 'doctrine
of manyness of reality'. Dr. Satkari Mookerjee expresses it as a
doctrine of 'non-absolutism'. This is also expressed as a theory of
'conditional predication' or 'theory of relativity of propositions.'

Since the doctrine of 'Anekäntaväd' is opposed to absolutism or monism
(Ekänta-väd), we would prefer a phrase 'doctrine of non-absolutism' to
convey the meaning of Anekäntaväd. The doctrine of Anekäntaväd can be
subdivided in two categories:

-   Nayaväd or Naya, which relates to thoughts and analysis.

-   Syädväd, which relates to speech.

What we know by the analytical process of Nayaväd, we express by the
synthesis of Syädväd. The base of both is knowledge.

As described earlier, according to Jainism, in order to have a complete
and comprehensive judgment of reality, one has to consider the main
substance that has the element of permanence and undergoes changes in
various forms. In this process of change, the previous form goes away,
and a new form comes into existence. The emergence of the new form is
called Utpäd (appearance), the elimination of the old form is called
Vyaya (disappearance), and the substance, which remains constant during
this process, is called Dhrauvya (permanence). When one is able to
comprehend all three, they can arrive at a proper judgment about the
thing in question.

For instance, when the self takes the form of a human being, you can
know it as a 'child' or an 'adult.' Such a description is true from the
standpoint of the form(s) that the self has assumed. Therefore, when we
recognize something from a point of view of the modification or change,
it is called 'Paryäyärthik Naya'. Paryäy means modification or change.
However, when we recognize that thing from a point of view of the
substance, it is called Dravyärthik Naya. In the former, mode is
predominant and substance subordinate, while in the latter, substance is
predominant and mode subordinate. That is, the former considers changing
aspects of reality, while the latter considers its permanent aspect.

The greatest contribution that Jainism has made to the world of thought,
is by its theories of Nayaväd and Syädväd. The word 'Syäd' in Sanskrit
means 'perhaps', but in Jainism it is used to show the relativity of a
judgment. The word 'Naya' means 'standpoint'.

Truth or reality is always complex and has many aspects.

If one is impressed by one of the aspects of a complex reality and
begins to identify that reality only by that aspect, they are bound to
make an incorrect judgment about reality. Therefore, the Jain seers urge
us to look at the complexities of life and knowledge from every
standpoint and from positive, as well as negative, aspects. They
recognize that an ordinary human being's comprehension is partial and
hence valid only from a particular point of view.

The complex reality has not only an infinite number of qualities, but
also an infinite number of relations. It may be looked at differently by
different people under different circumstances. It assumes different
forms and appearances for which due allowance ought to be made. All this
makes it difficult to form correct judgments without a systematic and
logical method. This method of identification is called Nayaväd.

According to the Jain logic, Naya becomes a form of false knowledge as
it determines the knowledge not of an object, but part of an object. The
knowledge of an object determined by Naya is valid knowledge from that
point of view. It does yield certain valid knowledge about part of the
object.

Dr. S. Rädhäkrishnan observes:

\"The doctrine of Naya is a peculiar feature of Jain logic. A Naya is a
standpoint from which we make a statement about a thing. What is true
from one standpoint may not be true from another. Particular aspects are
never adequate to the whole reality. The relative solutions are
abstractions under which reality may be regarded, but do not give us a
full and sufficient account of it. Jainism has a basic and fundamental
principle that truth is relative to our standpoint\".

If an object or theory is judged only from one standpoint, the judgment
is one sided and it is termed as 'Ekänt', As a reminder, 'Ek' means
'one' and 'Änta' means 'aspects' or 'attributes.' Thus, Ekänt means
one-sidedness. Jain principles ask us to judge any object from all
aspects, which is called 'Anekänt'.

Every fundamental principle of Jain philosophy is based on Anekänt,
which is able to accept the different or even contradictory aspects of
reality while developing a synthesis between the contradictory
philosophical theories.

If a comprehensive view of the whole reality is to be a comprehensive
perception of a thing, it is possible only when its permanent substance
(Dravya) is considered along with its existing mode (Paryäy). As Ächärya
Siddhasen states "we can understand a thing properly by perceiving its
various aspects".

A deeper understanding of Anekäntaväd and Syädväd provides great insight
into the problems of human interactions that cause conflict, grief,
envy, and hatred. It teaches us to be tolerant towards other viewpoints,
allowing us to live in harmony with people of different thinking.

Similarly, it is highly applicable in understanding social problems and
national strife. More importantly, these doctrines also provide ways of
resolving global differences and conflicts.

#### Classification of Naya

Jain philosophers have classified Naya in a few different ways. Based on
the ways in which we can perceive things, Naya can be broadly classified
into two types as follows:

##### Nishchay Naya (Absolute Point of View)

Here, one takes a substance, picks one of its attributes (Guna), and
analyzes one part of that attribute. This is called the absolute point
of view, e.g., to call a clay pot a form of clay, as it is made of clay.
Clay is the substance and one of its attributes is represented in the
form of a pot. The standpoint concentrating on the original pure nature
of a thing is called Nishchay Naya. It implies the real or the ultimate
meaning or interpretation of an object.

##### Vyavahär Naya (Practical Point of View)

The substance and its attributes are interdependent. Considering them
separately is called the practical point of view. For example, to know
is an attribute of the soul. To consider knowledge separately from the
soul is taking the practical point of view. Even though this practical
point of view is the incorrect way in which to know a substance,
day-to-day activities become somewhat easier utilizing it.

Example

We use a clay pot to hold water, so we now refer to it as a water pot.
Technically, the pot is not made of water, but clay. However, because of
water's association with the pot, we call it a water pot. The correct,
absolute description is that this pot is made of clay, and we use it to
store water, but this takes a long time and is impractical. By referring
to it as a water pot, we can convey the intended meaning and make future
tasks easier thereafter.

Similarly, even though the soul and body are separate, we use the words
interchangeably. We indicate the body as living because of its
association with the soul.

From the Nishchay Naya (absolute standpoint), a soul is uncontaminated
by matter and independent. From the Vyavahär standpoint, the soul can be
considered impure, as it is bound with Karma and trapped in the cycle of
birth and death. Such classification of Naya enables the identification
or distinction of objects or theories.

#### Seven main categories of Naya

An object can be described from a multiple point of view or Naya by
describing each of its many attributes or characteristics. Thus, there
can be many different types of Naya. However, the Jain Philosophers have
classified them into seven main categories.

  Sangrah Naya         Collective view

There are hundreds of further subclassifications, but this section will
consider the seven Naya at a high level. Before doing so, note that the
first three Naya are with reference to identifying the main substance or
'Dravya', and hence are known as 'Dravyärthik Naya'. The remaining four
refer to the standpoints identifying the modes of the main substance,
and hence are known as 'Paryäyärthik Naya'.

##### Dravyärthik Naya (Substantial Point of View)

Dravyärthik Naya refers to the standpoint that concentrates on a
substance. In this point of view, one considers the substance as a whole
and gives its modes subsidiary status. On the other hand, Paryäyärthik
Naya regards all things as impermanent, because they undergo changes
(transformations). Hence it declares that all things are non-eternal or
momentary from the standpoint of modes or changes.

Example:

While talking about the soul, one will consider the soul as immortal,
was never created nor will it ever be destroyed, but the body undergoes
changes into different forms. The standpoint that grasps the generic
aspect is Dravyärthik Naya. And the standpoint that grasps the specific
aspect is Paryäyärthik Naya.

Dravyärthik Naya can be subdivided as follows:

######  Naigam Naya (Generic Point of View)

The etymological meaning of the word Naigam is 'end product' or
'result.' Tattvärtha-sutra provides an illustration of a person carrying
water, rice, and fuel and who, when asked what he was doing, says he is
cooking. This reply is given in view of the result, which he intends to
achieve. However, at the exact time when the question is asked of him,
he is not actually cooking. His reply is correct from the Naigam Naya,
although technically it is not exactly correct because he is not
actually cooking at the time when he replies.

These empirical views proceed on the assumption that a thing possesses
the most generic, as well as the most specific qualities, and that we
stress any one of these at a given time and ignore the other. It
overlooks the distinction between the remote and the immediate, noting
one or the other as if it were the whole, depending upon the intention
of the observer.

For example, a man has decided to perform an act of theft. Religious
works regard him as defiled by the sin of theft, although he has not yet
performed the act of theft. The standpoint adopted is that the act,
which is sought to be undertaken is as good as being accomplished.

######  Sangrah Naya (Collective Point of View)

We imply Sangrah Naya when we emphasize some general class of
characteristics of a particular thing while ignoring the specific
characteristics of that class.

For example, assume there to be 500 people in a hall. Here we are now
considering only general qualities like people and not considering like
how many were adults, children, old, young, etc.

In another example, one may concentrate on a common quality found in all
souls, such as consciousness, and say that all souls are equal. Its
scope is more limited than Naigam Naya.

######  Vyavahär Naya (Practical Point of View)

If we consider something from Vyavahär Naya, we try to judge it from
specific properties while ignoring the generic qualities mainly
responsible for those specific qualities.

This point of view sees an object in its specific form, rather than its
general form. The practical view concentrates on the function of a thing
or being. It is analytic in its approach and often uses metaphors to
explain the nature of things. After describing things in a collective
form, it is" necessary to ascertain their specific characteristics.

For example, the word "medicine" includes all its subsidiary branches.
When one uses the terms allopathic, osteopathic, naturopathic,
homeopathic, etc. then we can understand which specialty of medicine
they are referring to. Such divisions are an example of a more
distributive point of view and have a tendency towards greater
specificity.

With understanding of Naigam Naya we should recognize the potentiality
of achieving liberation by all souls. As all souls are capable of
liberation, we should appreciate that potentiality in all souls. And we
show our respect and humbleness to all living beings. When we act
accordingly with all, this becomes Vyavahär Naya.

##### Paryäyärthik Naya (Modification Point of View)

As mentioned above, Paryäyärthik Naya (modification point of view)
regards all things as impermanent because they continuously undergo
changes (transformations). In this point of view, one primarily
considers the modes of a substance as a primary subject, while the
substantial consideration becomes secondary.

Example:

While talking about the soul, one may consider its ever-changing modes,
referring to the four realms (Gati) of existence, birth, growth, decay,
death, rebirth, etc.

######  Rujusutra Naya (Linear Point of View)

Narrower than Vyavahär in its outlook, Rujusutra Naya does not emphasize
all specific qualities but only those that appear at a particular
moment, ignoring their existent specific qualities of the past and the
future. The past and future modes of a thing are not real as they have
served or will serve their purpose and do not exist at the moment.

'Ruju' means simple, and 'sutra' means knowledge. If a man was king but
is no longer, his past is of no use in a linear point of view. The same
when applied to a person who will be a king in the future. The linear
point of view makes identification easier and the scope of a viewpoint
narrower.

######  Shabda Naya (Literal Point of View)

A verbalistic approach employs Shabda Naya. This standpoint maintains
that synonymous words convey the same meaning only if they are not
different in tense, case ending, gender, number, etc. That is, the
literal point of view uses words at their exact face value to signify
the real nature of things -- each word has a very particular meaning.
E.g., use of the words pot, and pitcher may signify the same meaning,
but in the following sentence their meanings differ: "Why did you bring
a pot? I only want a pitcher."

######  Samabhirudha Naya (Etymological Point of View)

It differs from Shabda Naya because it concentrates on the etymological
distinction between synonyms. It discards the conventional use of a word
in favor of the meaning derived from its root. The etymological view
asserts that, because the roots of synonyms are different, they are not
actually synonyms. A group of words may mean the same things in essence,
but as individual words, they might represent a special condition. Eg.
huts and palaces are places to live. However, poor people live in a hut
and king lives in a palace; in an etymological (historical or
derivation) point of view, it represents a specific quality of a word.

######  Evambhuta Naya (Determinant Point of View)

This Naya recognizes the actual action presently attributed to an
individual. In other words, only one word among synonyms correctly
correlates to a particular action.

In this point of view, the word or sentence, that further determines a
characteristic property in its present state is used. A word should be
used to denote its actual meaning, Eg. the word thief is to be used only
when a person is caught stealing and not because a person is a known
thief. It represents a strict application of a word or statement.

#### Summary of Naya

As already noted, the purpose of pointing out this detailed
classification of Naya is to show how different individuals can view the
same object from different perspectives. However, these different
aspects are only partially true. The analysis of Naya shows that every
judgment is relative to that particular aspect from which it is seen or
known. This is also called Säpeksha-väd, meaning our particular
knowledge or judgment is always relative to a particular standpoint.
Since human judgments are always from particular standpoints, their
outright acceptance as a sole truth or rejection as totally false would
not be correct. This led the Jain seers to their famous doctrine of
'Syädväd,' meaning the doctrine of relativity.

Nayaväd reveals a technique to arrive at such an understanding. If one
finds that a proposition is quite contrary to the conviction that one
had for the whole life and hence the cause of great irritation to him,
then after one applies the principles of Nayaväd, the irritation begins
to subside. The simple reason being that one begins to realize the real
cause for the contrary proposition.

### Syädväd or Sapta-Bhanga (Seven Predications)

Let us now understand what the theory of non-absolutism is as the Jain
theory of reality from its metaphysical point of view. The Jain approach
to ultimate reality can be expressed in two words: realistic and
relativistic. The universe is full of innumerable material atoms and
innumerable individual souls. They are separately and independently
real. Again, each thing and each soul possesses innumerable aspects of
its own. According to the metaphysical presupposition of Jainism, a
thing exists with infinite characteristics.

The theory of Anekäntaväd is the metaphysical theory of reality. Jainism
brings out another aspect of reality and that is its relativistic
pluralism. While Anekäntaväd explains reality metaphysically, Syädväd
explains it epistemologically (dealing with knowledge). Both are two
aspects of the same reality. We have already seen how human knowledge is
relative and limited which ultimately makes all our judgments relatively
or partially true, and not absolute.

Syädväd, or Sapta-bhanga Naya (sevenfold judgment), is known as the
theory of relativity of propositions or theory of relativity of
judgments. Some critics call it the theory of relativity of knowledge.
We can say that Syädväd is the epistemological explanation of reality,
while Sapta-bhanga Naya is the method behind the theory of sevenfold
judgment (the logical side of the theory).

All affirmations in some senses are true and in some senses are false.
Similarly, all assertions are indefinite and true in some sense as well
as indefinite and false in some other sense. Assertions could be true or
false or indefinite.

Thus, Jainism proposes to grant the non-absolute nature of reality and
relativistic pluralism of the object of knowledge by using the word
'Syät' (or Syäd) before the assertion or judgment.

The word 'Syät' literally means 'may be.' It is also translated as
'perhaps,' 'somehow,' 'relatively,' or 'in a certain sense.' Note that
the word 'Syät' is not used in the sense of probability leading to
uncertainty, since probability hints at skepticism

There are a good number of parables conventionally used by Jain writers
to explain the theory. The most famous one is the parable of six blind
men and the elephant. Each one was sure in his assertion alone being
correct and each one was correct from his point of view, though contrary
to the others.

Thus, Jains hold that affirmations will tell either about the existence,
or non-existence, or about the inexpressible. Combining the three will
give four more alternatives, thus deriving the seven alternatives
technically known as Sapta-bhanga Naya or the sevenfold Judgments.

#### Theory of Seven Predications (Sapta-Bhanga)

To clarify the above approach of ascertaining the truth by the process
of Syädväd, the Jain philosophers have evolved a formula of seven
predications, which are known as Sapta-bhanga. 'Sapta' means 'seven' and
'Bhanga' means 'mode'. These seven modes of ascertaining the truth can
be exact in exploring all possibilities and aspects.

For any proposition, there are three main modes of assessment, namely
(1) a positive assertion (Asti), (2) a negative assertion (Nästi), and
(3) an indescribable or non-expressible assertion (Avaktavya).

However, for greater clarity, four more permutations of these three are
added as: 'Asti-nästi,' 'Asti-avaktavya,' 'Nästi-avaktavya,' and
'Asti-nästi-avaktavya.' The word 'Syät' is prefixed to each of these
seven predications to prevent the proposition from being absolute.

All seven predications below are made with reference to an ethical
proposition that 'It is sin to commit violence.'

  ---------------------- -------------------------------------------------------------
  Asti                   It is sin to commit violence with an intention to commit the
                         same.

  Nästi                  It is not a sin to commit violence against an aggressor who
                         harms an innocent and helpless person.

  Asti-nästi             It is a sin to commit violence in breach of moral and social
                         laws, but it is not a sin if violence is required to be
                         committed in performance of moral or social duties.

  Avaktavya              It is not possible to say whether violence is a sin or virtue
                         without knowing the circumstances under which it is required
                         to be committed.

  Asti-avaktavya         Indeed, violence is sinful under certain circumstances, but
                         no positive statement of this type can be made at all times
                         and under all circumstances.

  Nästi-avaktavya        Violence is not indeed sinful under certain circumstances,
                         but no positive statement of this type can be made at all
                         times and under all circumstances.

  Asti-nästi-avaktavya   Violence is sinful, but there are circumstances where it is
                         not so. In fact, no statement in affirmation or negation can
                         be made at all times and all circumstances.
  ---------------------- -------------------------------------------------------------

Table K.2‑E

All these seven modes can be expressed with regard to every proposition.

This is the general view of the method of the Jain dialectic. Only this
type of dialectical method can represent Syädväd. The theory of
sevenfold predication is treated as synonymous with Syädväd since the
number of possible or alternative truths under the conditional method of
Syädväd is seven only.

At no time in the history of humanity is this principle of Syädväd more
necessary than in the present.

#### Theistic Implication of Syädväd

Understanding others' standpoints and learning to tolerate conflicting
or contrary situations helps towards the higher development of right
conduct. It broadens the mind and makes a person objective and open in
their thinking.

Syädväd, by molding a person towards better conduct and higher
knowledge, proves to be of great theistic significance. One of the aims
of life is to make the earth a better and worthier world. Syädväd, in
spite of its dry dialectic and forbidding use of logic, is not without a
lesson for the practical human beings of the world.

Pundit Dalsukhbhai Malvania, an authority on Jainism in one of his
essays explains that the most of Anekäntaväd is Ahimsä and that is the
prime reason that Jain philosophy is based on Anekäntaväd. The very idea
of not hurting others but to be kind and sympathetic towards others'
views and thus to be friendly, is the logical outcome of Ahimsä. Ahimsä
in its positive concept becomes love and compassion.

A perfect theism, not in its narrow rigid sense, but in the sense where
broad religiousness, deep spirituality, and high knowledge are thought
of for the soul's ultimate liberation from bondage, required Syädväd as
its valid approach to have an objective vision of truth, to be tolerant,
to be sympathetic, and to have an attitude of impartiality. Without all
these, no theism in its actual practice is possible. Syädväd shapes a
personality into a theistic one.

Moreover, subjective attitude and past recollections towards similar
objects play a decisive role in judgment. At the same time prejudices
and predilections, social upbringing, environmental necessities, and
politico-social taboos also play a very decisive role in a judgment
about an idea.

In fact, every object and every idea have infinite characteristics and
requires judgement from a variety of standpoints. What should be our
reaction towards a thing if we are convinced that everything in this
universe has infinite characteristics, and that with limited knowledge,
a human being is not capable of determining all these characteristics?
Certainly, if our approach were objective and unbiased, we would not
rush to take an absolute view of a thing by keeping in mind the
limitations of our knowledge.

Our judgment based on limited data is likely to be wrong. We would,
however, not have actual perception. Therefore, in our prudence, we
would say that the judgment formed about actually perceived things is
'likely' to be true. While saying so, we would not rule out the
possibility that it may turn out to be untrue if looked at from any
other perspective. This is the approach of Syädväd, which implies that
each and all knowledge is relative.

What we know by the analytical process of Nayaväd, we express by the
synthesis of Syädväd. As already noticed, the etymological meaning of
the word 'Syäd' is 'Perhaps'. However, it is used to suggest a relative
truth. Since our view has a limited aperture, we cannot know everything
and hence it is appropriate to avoid our absolute assertion.

For instance, when we say that a particular thing weighs 5 lb., our
statement about the weight is related to the gravitational force exerted
on that thing by our planet, the earth. The same thing may weigh nothing
if removed from this gravitational field or may weigh differently on a
different planet. The same can be said about our statements relating to
time and space and about every human experience.

It is the matter of our daily experience that the same object, which
gives pleasure to us under certain circumstances, becomes boring under
different circumstances.

Scientific truths are, therefore, relative in the sense that they do not
give complete and exhaustive knowledge of the objects under study and
contain elements that may be changed with further advance in knowledge.

Nonetheless, relative truth is undoubtedly useful as it is a
stepping-stone to the ultimate truth.

### Importance of Anekäntaväd

The importance of this comprehensive synthesis of 'Syädväd' and 'Anekänt
Naya' in day-to-day life is immense. These doctrines supply a rational
unification and synthesis of the manifold and reject the assertions of
bare absolutes.

Mahatma Gandhi's views (written in 1926) about the Jain theory of
Anekänt are as follows:

"It has been my experience that I am always true (correct) from my point
of view and often wrong from the point of view of my critics. I know
that we are both (I and my critics) right from our respective points of
view. I very much like this doctrine of the many viewpoints of the
reality. It is this doctrine that has taught me to judge a Muslim from
his standpoint and a Christian from his.\"

The history of all conflicts and confrontations in the world is the
history of intolerance born out of ignorance. Difficulty with the human
being is their egocentric existence.

Anekänt or Syädväd tries to make the human being conscious of their
limitation by pointing to ones narrow vision and limited knowledge of
the manifold aspects of things and asks him/her not to be hasty in
forming absolute judgments before examining various other aspects - both
positive and negative.

Obviously, much of the bloodshed, and much of tribulations of humanity
would have been saved if the human being had shown the wisdom of
understanding the contrary viewpoints.

### Anekäntaväd and Ahimsä

The important aspect of Anekäntaväd and Syädväd is the subtlety with
which it introduces the practice of Ahimsä (nonviolence) even in the
realm of thought. The moment one begins to consider the angle from which
a contrary viewpoint is put forward, one begins to develop tolerance,
which is the basic requirement of the practice of 'Ahimsä'. The origin
of all bloody wars fought on the surface of this earth can be traced to
the war of ideas, beliefs, and disagreements.

Anekäntaväd and Syädväd, both put a healing touch at the root of the
human psyche, try to stop the war of beliefs, which leads to the war of
nerves and then to the war of bloodshed. It makes all absolutes in the
field of thought quite irrelevant and naive, and it imparts maturity to
the thought process and supplies flexibility and originality to the
human mind.

If mankind will properly understand and adopt this doctrine of
Anekäntaväd and Syädväd, it will realize that real revolution was not
the French or Russian Revolutions; the real revolution was the one which
taught man to develop his/her power of understanding from all possible
aspects.
