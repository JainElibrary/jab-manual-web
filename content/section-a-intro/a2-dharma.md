# A.2. Dharma: Religion

### A.2.1. Introduction
The word "Dharma" is derived from the Sanskrit root *Dhri*, which means "to hold" or "to support." In a spiritual context, Dharma is that which supports the soul and prevents it from falling into a lower state of existence.

### A.2.2. What is Religion?
Religion is not just a set of rituals; it is a way of life. The path to liberation of right knowledge, right perception, and right conduct is the application of equanimity in the three aspects of our conscious life: knowing, feeling, and willingness.

Even-mindedness, a broader and unbiased outlook, and regard for other ideologies constitute equanimity of knowledge or right knowledge. Detachment from worldly pleasures and a feeling of equality constitute equanimity of feeling or right perception. Control over desires and equity in social life constitute equanimity of will or right conduct.

According to Jain teachers, equanimity of body, speech, and mind should be the directive principle:
- **Equanimity of mind** entails non-attachment or non-possessiveness.
- **Equanimity of body** is nonviolence (Ahimsä).
- **Equanimity of speech** is non-absolutism (Anekäntaväd).

### A.2.3. Understanding Jainism
Jainism is a religion propounded by a 'Jina'. The word 'Jina' means the conqueror or the victorious, referring to one who has conquered worldly passions like desire, hatred, anger, greed, and pride through strenuous personal effort.

Jina are not supernatural beings or incarnations of an all-powerful God, but people of this world who attained supreme knowledge and self-control. Jainism is therefore a religion of purely human origin, propagated by self-realized individuals liberated from the cycle of future life and death.

In ancient times, Jainism was also known as **Shraman Dharma** (an ascetic tradition) or the religion of **Nirgrantha** (one who is not attached to internal or external objects). The basic tenet is "Ahimsä Parmo Dharma," meaning non-violence is the supreme religion.

### A.2.4. Why Do We Pray?
A Jain verse says: “I bow down to the path of salvation, which is supreme, which is omniscient; I bow down to that power because I wish to become like that power”.

The object of prayer is not to receive material favors or to please a deity, but to absorb virtues and change one’s own personality to reflect those of the Tirthankars. We pay homage to the perfect for the sake of perfection. The concept of God as a creator, protector, or destroyer of the universe does not exist in Jainism.
